Sound for CBSE Class 9: Wave Properties, Echo and Human Ear

Published on October 25, 2026

Sound is a fascinating topic in CBSE Class 9 Science that connects everyday experiences with fundamental physics concepts. As per the NCERT syllabus (Chapter 12: Sound), students must understand how sound travels, its properties, and how our ears perceive it. This comprehensive guide will help you master sound concepts for your board exams and competitive entrance tests.

Understanding Sound Waves and Their Properties

Sound is a form of energy that travels in the form of waves. According to NCERT, sound requires a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to propagate. Unlike light, sound cannot travel through a vacuum, which is why space is silent.

Key Wave Properties of Sound:

  • Wavelength (λ): Distance between consecutive compressions or rarefactions. Measured in meters.
  • Frequency (f): Number of oscillations per second, measured in Hertz (Hz). Human hearing range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement of particles from their mean position. Determines loudness of sound.
  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation. T = 1/f
  • Velocity of Sound (v): Speed at which sound travels. Formula: v = f × λ

A critical exam point: the velocity of sound is constant in a given medium. In air at 25°C, sound travels at approximately 343 m/s. Students often confuse this with frequency and wavelength, which can vary. Remember that when frequency increases in the same medium, wavelength must decrease proportionally to maintain constant velocity.

Echo: NCERT Chapter 12 Application

Echo is a repetition of sound caused by reflection from a distant object. This concept appears frequently in CBSE Class 9 board exams and carries 2-3 marks regularly.

Conditions for Hearing Echo:

  • Minimum distance between source and reflecting surface should be 17 meters in air at room temperature
  • Time interval between original sound and echo should be at least 0.1 seconds for the human ear to distinguish them
  • Sound must be loud enough to reflect and travel back

Calculation formula for echo distance:

Distance = (Velocity of Sound × Time Interval) / 2

The division by 2 accounts for sound traveling to the reflecting surface and back. This numerical problem format is extremely common in CBSE exams. Practice solving: "A person stands 680 m away from a cliff. If the velocity of sound is 340 m/s, will the person hear an echo? Calculate the time interval." (Answer: Yes, time = 4 seconds)

⚠️ Common Exam Mistake

Students often forget to divide by 2 when calculating echo distance. The distance formula includes the return journey, so many forget this critical factor. Always remember: sound travels to the object AND back, hence the division by 2.

The Human Ear: Structure and Function

NCERT Chapter 12 dedicates significant content to human ear anatomy and auditory perception. Understanding ear structure is essential for board exams, particularly for 3-4 mark questions.

Three Main Divisions of the Human Ear:

1. Outer Ear (Pinna and Ear Canal):

  • Pinna: External structure that collects sound waves
  • Auditory Canal: Directs sound to the eardrum (tympanum)

2. Middle Ear (Ossicles):

  • Contains three tiny bones: Hammer (Malleus), Anvil (Incus), and Stirrup (Stapes)
  • These amplify vibrations and transmit them to the inner ear
  • Eustachian tube equalizes air pressure

3. Inner Ear (Cochlea):

  • Spiral-shaped structure containing fluid
  • Contains hair cells that convert vibrations to electrical signals
  • Auditory nerve transmits signals to the brain
  • Brain interprets these signals as sound

Hearing Range and Sound Classification:

Sound frequencies are classified as:

  • Infrasound: Less than 20 Hz (inaudible to humans but sensed by animals)
  • Audible Sound: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (human hearing range)
  • Ultrasound: Greater than 20,000 Hz (used in medical imaging and pest control)

CBSE exams frequently ask: "Why can't humans hear infrasound?" or "How is ultrasound used in hospitals?" Practice these applications as they connect theory to real-world scenarios.

📚 Exam-Focused Tip

When answering ear structure questions, use labeled diagrams. CBSE values step-by-step explanations. Mention each part's function briefly. For example: "Pinna collects sound → Ear canal directs it → Eardrum vibrates → Ossicles amplify → Cochlea converts to signals → Brain perceives sound." This systematic approach scores full marks.

Sound Properties and Practical Applications

Beyond theory, NCERT emphasizes practical understanding of sound behavior and its applications in daily life and technology.

Reflection of Sound: Sound reflects from hard surfaces like walls, cliffs, and metal sheets. This principle is used in concert halls, movie theaters, and medical ultrasound imaging. The NCERT curriculum expects students to understand that reflected sound follows the law of reflection: angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.

Absorption of Sound: Soft materials like foam, curtains, and carpets absorb sound. Concert halls and recording studios use acoustic panels for sound absorption. This prevents unwanted echoes and reverberations.

Loudness and Intensity: While loudness is subjective (depends on perception), intensity is objective and measured in decibels (dB). Conversation: 60 dB, Heavy traffic: 80 dB, Rock concert: 110 dB, Pain threshold: 130 dB. Exposure to sounds above 80-90 dB can cause hearing damage over time. CBSE often includes health-related questions on this topic.

For board exams, remember: Loudness ∝ Amplitude and Pitch ∝ Frequency. A thick string produces low pitch (low frequency), while a thin string produces high pitch (high frequency). This concept bridges sound theory with everyday musical instruments.
