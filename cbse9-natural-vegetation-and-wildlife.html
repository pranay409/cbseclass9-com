Natural Vegetation and Wildlife for CBSE Class 9: Complete Notes

November 19, 2026

Natural Vegetation and Wildlife is a crucial chapter in CBSE Class 9 Geography (Chapter 5, Social Science). This chapter explores Earth's diverse ecosystems, the plants and animals that inhabit them, and the delicate balance that sustains life. Understanding this topic is essential for board exams, state-level tests, and competitive examinations.

The chapter introduces students to the concept of natural vegetation—plants that grow naturally without human intervention—and how they vary across different climatic zones and regions of India. It also covers wildlife diversity and conservation efforts, connecting environmental science with practical real-world applications.

Understanding Natural Vegetation and Its Types

Natural vegetation refers to plant communities that develop and thrive in natural conditions without human management or cultivation. It is primarily determined by climate, soil, and topography.

Major Types of Natural Vegetation:

  • Forests: Dense collections of trees covering large areas. Types include tropical rainforests, deciduous forests, and coniferous forests.
  • Grasslands: Areas dominated by grasses with few trees. Examples include savannas, prairies, and steppes.
  • Shrublands: Regions with low-growing shrubs, common in semi-arid climates like the Mediterranean region.
  • Tundra: Cold, treeless vegetation found in Arctic regions with mosses and lichens.
  • Deserts: Sparse vegetation adapted to extremely dry conditions, including cacti and drought-resistant plants.

Each type of vegetation supports specific wildlife and plays a vital role in maintaining Earth's ecological balance. For CBSE exams, understanding the relationship between climate and vegetation types is fundamental—tropical climates support dense forests, while arid regions support sparse desert vegetation.

India's Natural Vegetation and Wildlife Biodiversity

India is one of the world's megadiverse countries, home to numerous ecosystems and species. The NCERT curriculum emphasizes India-specific examples to help students relate to their own environment.

Major Vegetation Zones in India:

  • Tropical Rainforests: Found in the Western Ghats and northeastern regions (Assam, Meghalaya). Characterized by high rainfall, dense canopy, and rich biodiversity. Home to elephants, tigers, and numerous bird species.
  • Deciduous Forests: Cover much of the Indian peninsula. These forests shed leaves during dry seasons (winter and summer). Found in central and northern India.
  • Dry Deciduous Forests: Located in areas with moderate rainfall (50-100 cm annually). Common in northwestern and central India.
  • Himalayan Vegetation: Includes coniferous forests at higher altitudes, transitioning to alpine vegetation and tundra at the highest elevations. Home to snow leopards, musk deer, and alpine flowers.
  • Grasslands and Meadows: Found in Himalayan foothills and elevated plateaus. Important habitats for grazing animals.

Wildlife of India:

India supports an astonishing diversity of wildlife:

  • Mammals: Tigers, Asian elephants, one-horned rhinoceros, leopards, bears, monkeys, and deer species.
  • Birds: Peacocks, eagles, owls, flamingos, and numerous migratory species.
  • Reptiles: Snakes (cobras, pythons), crocodiles, and monitors lizards.
  • Amphibians and Fish: Frogs, salamanders, and freshwater fish species.

⚠️ Important Exam Tip

Common Mistake: Students often confuse "natural vegetation" with "cultivated crops." Remember: natural vegetation grows without human intervention (forests, grasslands), while crops are deliberately planted and managed by humans. The NCERT explicitly distinguishes these concepts—this distinction frequently appears in MCQs and short-answer questions on CBSE exams.

Biodiversity Hotspots and Conservation in India

India contains four recognized biodiversity hotspots—regions with exceptional concentrations of endemic species:

  • Western Ghats: One of the world's eight biodiversity hotspots, with unique tropical rainforest ecosystems and thousands of endemic plant and animal species.
  • Eastern Himalayas: Alpine forests and unique mountain ecosystems supporting rare species like the red panda and snow leopard.
  • Sundarbans: The world's largest mangrove forest, home to the Bengal tiger and numerous aquatic species.
  • Indo-Burma Region: Covering northeastern India with high endemism rates.

Wildlife Protection and Conservation:

To protect endangered species and habitats, India has established:

  • National Parks: Areas of outstanding natural beauty where wildlife is protected. Examples include Jim Corbett National Park, Gir National Park, and Kaziranga National Park.
  • Wildlife Sanctuaries: Areas designated for wildlife conservation with restricted human activities. The Periyar Wildlife Sanctuary and Sundarbans Wildlife Sanctuary are notable examples.
  • Biosphere Reserves: Regions combining protected areas with sustainable development. India has 18 biosphere reserves, including Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve and Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve.

The NCERT curriculum emphasizes India's commitment to conservation through legislation like the Wildlife Protection Act (1972) and Project Tiger, launched to protect endangered tiger populations. For exam purposes, students should remember at least 3-4 national parks and their flagship species.

Threats to Natural Vegetation and Wildlife

Modern human activities threaten India's natural heritage:

  • Deforestation: Clearing forests for agriculture, urbanization, and industrial development. This destroys habitats and reduces biodiversity.
  • Habitat Fragmentation: Roads, dams, and settlements break continuous ecosystems into isolated patches.
  • Poaching and Hunting: Illegal hunting of endangered species for fur, tusks, and other body parts.
  • Pollution: Industrial waste and chemicals contaminate soil, water, and air, harming vegetation and wildlife.
  • Climate Change: Altering precipitation patterns and temperatures, affecting species' survival and migration patterns.
  • Invasive Species: Non-native plants and animals disrupting local ecosystems.

CBSE exams frequently ask students to discuss these threats and suggest conservation measures. Practice answering questions like: "What are the threats to wildlife in India?" or "How does deforestation affect biodiversity?"
